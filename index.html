Four flower silhouettes & the botany of petal counts

6 petals

The Sexfoil

Trimerous monocots · lilies & kin

Count six and you're almost certainly looking at a monocot: lilies, tulips, amaryllis, crocus and daffodils all build their blooms in threes, doubled into a six-part whorl.

Botanists often call these parts tepals rather than petals, because the inner and outer rings look identical — the sepals have dressed up to match the petals exactly.

Fun fact: The "Star of David" sexfoil and the six-petalled rosette are among the oldest decorative motifs in human art, carved long before anyone named the monocots.

5 petals

The Cinquefoil

Pentamerous symmetry · Rosaceae & friends

Five is nature's favourite number. Roses, apple and cherry blossoms, buttercups, wild geraniums and strawberry flowers all open in fives — the hallmark of the eudicots and especially the rose family, Rosaceae.

This pentaradial arrangement isn't accidental: five and its multiples sit squarely in the Fibonacci sequence that shapes so much plant growth, packing petals and seeds with elegant efficiency.

Fun fact: Slice an apple crosswise and you'll find a five-pointed star of seed pockets — a fossil of the blossom's five-petal plan.

4 petals

The Quatrefoil

Cruciform · Brassicaceae

Four petals arranged in a cross is the signature of the mustard and cabbage family, Brassicaceae — once aptly called the Cruciferae, the "cross-bearers." Broccoli, radish, canola and wild mustard all flash this tidy plus-sign.

Beyond the brassicas, dogwoods and evening primroses wear the quatrefoil too, and the shape became a beloved motif in Gothic windows and medieval tracery.

Fun fact: Those four "petals" on a flowering dogwood are actually bracts — modified leaves — while the true flowers cluster, tiny, in the centre.

3 petals

The Trefoil

Trimerous baseline · Liliopsida

Three is the foundational number of the monocots. Irises, trilliums, spiderworts and many orchids show their petals in a clean threefold whorl — the simplest expression of monocot architecture.

The trefoil also escaped the garden into symbolism: the heraldic shamrock, the architect's trefoil arch, and emblems of trinity all borrow this three-lobed silhouette.

Fun fact: A trillium's name says it all — tri- everything: three petals, three sepals, and three leaves in a single elegant tier.
